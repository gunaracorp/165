# 5. ETHICAL DUTY

Setiap covered person wajib:

- bertindak jujur,
- mengungkapkan conflict of interest,
- menjaga kerahasiaan yang sah,
- menghormati rights,
- tidak memalsukan evidence,
- tidak menyalahgunakan aset,
- tidak menggunakan kedudukan untuk keuntungan pribadi yang tidak sah,
- dan melaporkan risiko serius.

---

# 6. CONFLICT OF INTEREST DEFINITION

Conflict of interest terjadi ketika kepentingan pribadi, keluarga, finansial, profesional, institusional, atau hubungan lain dapat memengaruhi—atau secara wajar terlihat dapat memengaruhi—keputusan seseorang atas nama 165.

Conflict dapat berupa:

### ACTUAL
Konflik nyata dan sedang terjadi.

### POTENTIAL
Konflik dapat terjadi karena hubungan atau kepentingan yang ada.

### PERCEIVED
Kondisi yang secara wajar dapat terlihat sebagai konflik walaupun pihak tersebut merasa objektif.

Ketiganya perlu ditangani secara proporsional.

---

# 7. EXAMPLES OF CONFLICT

Contoh:

- vendor dimiliki oleh keluarga decision-maker,
- editor memperoleh keuntungan dari publikasi yang sedang dinilai,
- researcher memiliki commercial interest pada hasil penelitian,
- sponsor meminta perlakuan editorial khusus,
- contributor memiliki kepentingan hukum terhadap archive yang ingin dipublikasikan,
- board member memiliki hubungan finansial dengan potential partner,
- founder mempunyai kepentingan pribadi pada keputusan bisnis tertentu.

---

# 8. CONFLICT DISCLOSURE

Setiap material conflict harus:

1. diidentifikasi,
2. diungkapkan,
3. dicatat,
4. dinilai,
5. dikelola.

Disclosure bukan pengakuan wrongdoing.

Tujuannya adalah memungkinkan keputusan yang dapat dipercaya.

# 10. MATERIALITY

Tidak semua conflict membutuhkan tindakan yang sama.

### LOW

Tidak memiliki kemungkinan material memengaruhi keputusan.

### MODERATE

Dapat memengaruhi atau menimbulkan perception issue.

### HIGH

Dapat secara material memengaruhi integrity, finance, research, rights, archive, governance, or reputation.

High conflict harus memiliki documented management action.

---

# 11. CONFLICT MANAGEMENT OPTIONS

Pilihan:

- disclosure only,
- consultation,
- recusal,
- independent review,
- reassignment,
- competitive procurement,
- dual approval,
- governance review,
- external review,
- removal from decision.

Conflict management harus proporsional terhadap risiko.

---

# 12. RECUSAL

Pihak yang conflicted dapat diwajibkan untuk tidak:

- approve,
- vote,
- select vendor,
- determine editorial outcome,
- determine rights decision,
- determine funding allocation,

pada keputusan yang affected.

Recusal harus dicatat.

---

# 13. FOUNDER CONFLICTS

Founder dapat memiliki founder-specific conflicts.

Founder status tidak menghapus kebutuhan disclosure.

Jika founder memiliki material personal or financial interest:

- disclose,
- identify affected decision,
- use appropriate independent review,
- document outcome.

Untuk reserved powers yang memang secara konstitusional melekat pada founder, conflict management harus tetap mempertahankan lawful authority sambil meningkatkan transparency.

---

# 14. BOARD / GOVERNING BODY CONFLICTS

Board members harus mengungkapkan material interests.

Meeting records should note:

- disclosure,
- recusal,
- remaining decision-makers,
- final decision.

---

# 15. EDITORIAL CONFLICTS

Editor/reviewer harus disclose bila:

- menulis source yang sedang dinilai,
- mempunyai personal dispute,
- memiliki financial stake,
- memiliki direct institutional interest.

Editorial independence must be protected.

---

# 16. RESEARCH CONFLICTS

Researchers harus disclose:

- funding relationship,
- institutional affiliation,
- authorship relationship,
- family relationship to subject,
- financial interest,
- commissioned research.

Disclosure does not invalidate research.

It allows readers/reviewers to evaluate context.

---

# 17. ARCHIVE CONFLICTS

Archive decisions can create conflicts when:

- custodian requests favorable description,
- donor conditions access,
- family requests suppression,
- institution controls rights,
- archive staff personally own related materials.

Rights and provenance must remain separate from personal preference.

---

# 18. TECHNOLOGY CONFLICTS

Technology decisions require disclosure when:

- decision-maker receives vendor compensation,
- decision-maker owns vendor equity,
- preferred vendor is a related party,
- architecture is chosen to create lock-in benefiting the decision-maker.

Critical technology choices should consider:

- interoperability,
- exit cost,
- security,
- data export,
- total cost of ownership.

---

# 19. PROCUREMENT ETHICS

Procurement must be:

- documented,
- proportionate,
- conflict-aware,
- evidence-based.

For material procurement, consider:

- multiple quotes where practical,
- vendor capability,
- security,
- contractual rights,
- data ownership,
- service continuity,
- exit strategy.

---

# 20. GIFTS & HOSPITALITY

Gifts or hospitality must not influence editorial, procurement, research, archive, partnership, or governance decisions.

165 should define thresholds once a formal entity and finance system exist.

Potential treatment:

- trivial and customary → may be allowed,
- material → disclose,
- influential/conditional → decline or return.

No person should accept benefits in exchange for favorable treatment.

---

# 21. SPONSORSHIP ETHICS

Sponsors may support:

- preservation,
- research,
- education,
- events,
- technology,
- production,
- public programs.

Sponsors may not purchase:

- historical conclusions,
- favorable research findings,
- fabricated authority,
- preferential editorial treatment,
- suppression of evidence.

---

# 22. SPONSORSHIP DISCLOSURE

Where material, disclose:

- sponsor identity,
- nature of support,
- relevant program,
- whether sponsor has editorial control.

Example:

> “This digitization project was supported by [Sponsor]. Sponsor funding does not determine historical interpretation.”

---

# 23. DONATION ETHICS

Donations should not create hidden authority.

A donor may support a mission.

Donation must not automatically create:

- editorial control,
- historical control,
- archive ownership,
- governance seat,
- special spiritual status.

Material donor arrangements should be documented.

---

# 24. DONOR RESTRICTIONS

Restricted donations may be accepted only when restrictions:

- are lawful,
- are administratively practical,
- do not compromise mission,
- do not compromise editorial independence,
- do not create unacceptable reputational or operational risk.

165 may reject a donation when conditions are incompatible with mission or ethics.

---

# 25. REVENUE INTEGRITY

Revenue generation must not depend on deception.

Never monetize through:

- fabricated historical claims,
- fabricated spiritual promises,
- fake credentials,
- invented lineage,
- manufactured testimonials,
- manipulated archive evidence.

- spiritual initiation,

Commercial products may include:

- books,

# 27. NO PAY-TO-LEGITIMACY

Payment must not determine:

- historical legitimacy,
- lineage status,
- scholarly truth,
- spiritual legitimacy,
- institutional recognition.

A wealthy donor does not gain historical truth authority by virtue of funding.

# 34. PAYMENTS TO CONTRIBUTORS

- scope,
- rate,
- rights,
- attribution,

Payment does not buy factual approval.

---

# 35. COMMISSIONED RESEARCH

When research is commissioned:

- funder is disclosed when appropriate,
- research scope is documented,
- methodology remains credible,
- conclusions are not predetermined,
- conflicts are disclosed.

---

# 36. RESEARCH FUNDING

Funding agreements should not require researchers to produce predetermined conclusions.

Where funding conditions materially constrain methodology or publication, disclose limitations where appropriate.

---

# 37. ARCHIVE DONATIONS

For donated or deposited archives, written terms should define:

- ownership,
- custody,
- license,
- access,
- reproduction,
- withdrawal conditions,
- preservation responsibilities,
- attribution.

---

# 38. DATA ETHICS

Data must be collected only for legitimate purposes.

Principles:

- data minimization,
- purpose limitation,
- appropriate security,
- controlled access,
- retention discipline,
- transparent notice,
- lawful handling.

---

# 39. PERSONAL DATA

Do not collect personal data simply because technology permits it.

Collect what is necessary for legitimate operations.

Special care for:

- living people,
- minors,
- private correspondence,
- family records,
- sensitive identity information.

---

# 40. ARCHIVE PRIVACY

Historical value does not automatically remove privacy considerations.

For living persons, assess:

- necessity,
- public interest,
- sensitivity,
- consent,
- legal requirements,
- risk.

---

# 41. CONSENT

When collecting:

- oral history,
- photographs of private individuals,
- personal documents,
- recordings,

obtain informed consent where required or appropriate.

Consent should address:

- purpose,
- publication,
- access,
- reuse,
- language/translation,
- preservation,
- withdrawal limitations.

---

# 42. CONSENT & PRESERVATION

If an item enters a preservation archive under terms that limit public access, the preservation record should survive even when public publication is restricted.

---

# 43. CHILDREN & VULNERABLE PERSONS

Extra safeguards apply to materials involving:

- children,
- vulnerable people,
- trauma,
- private family matters,
- sensitive personal circumstances.

Minimum necessary disclosure applies.

---

# 44. AI ETHICS

AI must operate under:

- human accountability,
- source traceability,
- privacy controls,
- access controls,
- bias awareness,
- security,
- correction,
- auditability.

AI must not be presented as an independent spiritual or historical authority.

---

# 45. AI USE DISCLOSURE

Disclosure may be appropriate when AI materially assists:

- transcription,
- translation,
- image restoration,
- categorization,
- research synthesis,
- content generation.

Internal records should retain sufficient information for audit where appropriate.

---

# 46. AI TRAINING DATA

Before using sensitive or restricted 165 archives for AI training:

- verify rights,
- verify consent/permissions,
- classify access,
- define purpose,
- assess leakage risk,
- establish withdrawal/retention procedure where applicable.

Private archive data must not become public training data by accident.

---

# 47. AI AND PERSONAL DATA

Do not upload unnecessary personal/sensitive archival data to third-party AI systems.

For external AI services, assess:

- data retention,
- provider terms,
- access,
- training use,
- regional/legal exposure,
- deletion capability.

---

# 48. SECURITY ETHICS

Anyone with privileged access must:

- use individual credentials,
- protect authentication factors,
- avoid credential sharing,
- report suspected compromise,
- follow least privilege.

No employee or founder should normalize unsafe security practices for convenience.

---

# 49. WHISTLEBLOWING / REPORTING

165 should provide a route to report:

- fraud,
- fabricated evidence,
- serious conflict,
- harassment,
- rights abuse,
- privacy abuse,
- security misconduct,
- misuse of funds,
- deliberate editorial manipulation.

Good-faith reporting must not result in retaliation.

---

# 50. REPORTING CHANNEL

# 51. RETALIATION PROHIBITION

No person may be punished solely for making a good-faith report.

Retaliation itself should be treated as an ethical/governance concern.

False accusations knowingly made with malicious intent are treated separately.

# 53. CONFIDENTIALITY

Reports should be handled confidentially to the extent practical.

# 65. REPATRIATION / RETURN CONCERNS

Before accepting a major partnership:

Does it fit 165?

### Integrity
Could it compromise independence?

### Rights
Who owns what?

### Data
What data is shared?

### Finance
What obligations arise?

### Reputation
Could relationship create material risk?

---

# 67. SPONSORSHIP ACCEPTANCE TEST

Ask:

```text
Does funding support the mission?
Can editorial independence remain?
Can sponsor relationship be disclosed?
Are conditions acceptable?
Can the project continue if sponsor exits?
```

Transparency must not mean:

> publish everything.

> **Publish what the public needs to evaluate institutional integrity while protecting legitimate privacy and security.**

- credentials,
- private keys,
- security configurations that create exploitation risk,
- private personal information.

- controls,
- policies,
- governance,
- incident process.

# 97. DECISION QUALITY QUESTIONS

Before major decision:

### Integrity
Would we be comfortable explaining this publicly?

### Independence
Could an outsider reasonably see undue influence?

### Evidence
Is decision based on verifiable information?

### Rights
Are we respecting ownership and privacy?

### Mission
Does this protect 165?

### Incentives
Could this create perverse incentives?

### Reversibility
Can we unwind it?

### Continuity
Would the decision still make sense in ten years?

---

# 98. “SUNLIGHT TEST”

For material decisions, ask:

> **Would we make the same decision if the source of funding, relationship, and reasoning were later made public?**

If not, pause and review.

---

# 99. “FUTURE HISTORIAN TEST”

Ask:

> **How would a future historian interpret this decision if they discovered the full record 50 years later?**

This is a useful discipline for an institution whose mission itself is preservation.

---

# 100. ZERO-TOLERANCE AREAS

Subject to applicable law and formal due process, 165 treats the following as severe integrity violations:

- fabrication,
- deliberate source falsification,
- fabricated genealogy,
- fabricated quotation,
- fraud,
- deliberate financial concealment,
- bribery for editorial treatment,
- intentional privacy abuse,
- unauthorized sale/transfer of core archives,
- deliberate manipulation of institutional metrics,
- retaliation against good-faith reporting,
- impersonation of institutional/spiritual authority.

---

# 101. FAIR PROCESS

Ethical allegations must be assessed fairly.

A report is not proof.

The accused should not automatically be declared guilty before evidence is assessed.

Material decisions should be based on documented findings.

---

# 102. CORRECTIVE ACTION

Actions may include:

- coaching,
- training,
- process correction,
- recusal,
- access restriction,
- removal from role,
- contract action,
- repayment/recovery,
- governance action,
- legal escalation where appropriate.

Response should be proportional.

---

# 103. RECORD RETENTION

Ethics and conflict records should be retained according to:

- legal requirements,
- operational need,
- privacy principles,
- preservation value.

Sensitive records require controlled access.

---

# 104. CONFIDENTIALITY OF COI RECORDS

The public need not see all personal details.

Public disclosure should reveal institutional accountability without unnecessarily exposing private information.

---

# 105. ETHICAL CULTURE

165 should reward:

- disclosure,
- correction,
- careful disagreement,
- transparency,
- good stewardship,
- evidence,
- respectful treatment.

It should discourage:

- hidden influence,
- fear-based control,
- hero worship as governance,
- financial secrecy,
- citation theater,
- manipulation.

---

# 106. NO HERO SYSTEM

165 should not design governance around:

> “Founder is always right.”

Nor:

> “Board is always right.”

Nor:

> “Researcher is always right.”

Nor:

> “AI is always right.”

Institutional trust comes from:

> **evidence + role clarity + review + accountability + documented process.**

---

# 107. MISSION SAFEGUARD

No financial relationship may override:

- Constitution,
- Operating Constitution,
- Founder Charter,
- Editorial Constitution,
- Source & Citation Policy,
- Correction/Retraction Policy.

If a commercial arrangement conflicts materially with foundational principles, mission protection takes precedence.

# 108. ETHICS & MEMBERSHIP

Digital membership benefits must not create:

- spiritual privilege,
- historical privilege for sale,
- preferential truth status,
- authority to represent TQN.

Any paid digital benefit must be a legitimate service or utility.

# 109. ETHICS & DONOR WALLS

- scholarly authority,
- spiritual rank,
- institutional office,
- ownership of heritage.
